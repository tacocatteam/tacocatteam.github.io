LESSON 01 · OBSERVE

Where does rain go after it lands?

On soil, some rain can soak in. On pavement or a roof, much more water moves across the surface. That moving water is called runoff. It can pick up soil, litter, fertilizer, or salt on its way downhill.

NOTICEWhere does the water start?
TRACEWhich way does it move?
QUESTIONWhat might it carry?
Rain→Hard surface→Runoff→Waterway

Think about it: How would the route change if some water could soak into a planted area first?

LESSON 02 · DESIGN

Build a place for rain to pause.

A rain garden is a shallow planted area designed to receive some runoff. Water spreads out, slows down, and soaks into suitable soil. Native plants can add habitat for local insects and other wildlife.

SHAPEA shallow basin, not a pond.
SOILDrainage must suit the site.
PLANTSChoose species suited to wet and dry periods.

Design challenge

Why might a garden beside a downspout work better than one where no runoff reaches it? What else should you check before digging?

Reveal a strong answer

The garden needs an appropriate runoff source, but its location also needs safe distance from buildings and buried utilities, suitable drainage, and enough room for people to pass.

REVIEW

Check your thinking.

A rain garden is the same as a permanent pond. True or false?

False. A rain garden is designed to hold water temporarily and drain between storms.

One model test proves a garden will work everywhere. True or false?

False. A full garden depends on its location, soil, runoff, and maintenance. Repeated tests provide stronger evidence.

Native plants can help local wildlife. True or false?

True. Species suited to the local area can provide food and shelter for wildlife.
